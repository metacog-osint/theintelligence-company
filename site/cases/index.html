Noisefloor: GPS loss over California during a declared Navy test, September 2026

The published finding. On 22 Sep 2026 Noisefloor recorded two short bursts of aircraft reporting degraded GPS over the Bay Area and the Central Valley, at 18:00 UTC to 18:08 UTC (peak 13 aircraft) and 19:52 UTC to 20:12 UTC (peak 36), on a day of quiet space weather (Kp no higher than 1.7). It is one of the checks on Noisefloor's validation page ↗.

The source it was checked against. FAA flight advisory PMSRCA GPS 26-03, Revision 2 ↗ scheduled GPS interference testing on the Point Mugu Sea Range, centred at 34°21′28″N 121°41′32″W, off the coast of central California. GPS, including WAAS and ADS-B, "may not be available" within 567 NM at 10,000 ft and 623 NM at FL250. The test was scheduled for 15 to 17 Sep, daily from 18:30 to 22:29 UTC, and 22 to 24 Sep, daily from 18:30 to 20:29 UTC. The advisory says each event may last the whole period; it does not say that every scheduled day was used.

Every scheduled day, and the days around them

Checked again on 24 Nov 2026 from Noisefloor's published daily burst files. The table counts burst events (several regions at once grouped into one event) whose centre is within 1,050 km (567 NM) of the test centre. Times are shown in your time zone; the advisory's own times are UTC.

What this establishes

  • On five of the six scheduled days, aircraft over California reported degraded GPS inside the declared test hours. On each of those five days the largest event was bigger than anything in the same hours on the four days without a test (largest 17 aircraft).
  • The timing comes from aircraft broadcasts alone, collected by a volunteer receiver network, and was matched to a schedule that was published in advance.
  • The space weather was quiet, so a geomagnetic storm does not explain it.

What it does not establish

  • That the test caused any one burst. A schedule that lines up is consistent with the test; it does not show which transmitter was on, or when.
  • Why the burst at 18:00 UTC on 22 Sep began before the window opened, or why the days without a test are not empty. Both stay open.
  • Why 16 Sep shows nothing. The test may not have run that day; the advisory does not say.
  • How many aircraft were affected in all. The counts are a lower bound: they include only aircraft the receiver network heard, after Noisefloor's filters. An independent account counted about 40 and 80 aircraft above 8,000 ft in the two 22 Sep bursts.
  • The 22 Sep file was built with the earlier within-day burst threshold; the other days use the threshold from the same time slot on the previous 14 days, so counts are not exactly comparable between days.

  • The advisory: the FAA PDF linked above, from FAASafety.gov.
  • Aircraft data: adsb.lol ↗ (ODbL). Space weather: Kp from GFZ Potsdam (CC BY 4.0).

Vigil: a medical-imaging tool added to CISA's list of exploited flaws, November 2026

The published finding. Vigil published vigil:kev:CVE-2026-133968: "CVE-2026-133968 (Project MONAI MONAI Label) added to CISA's KEV catalog on 23 Nov 2026; EPSS 0.04", and beside it vigil:adp:CVE-2026-133968, CISA's own assessment that exploitation is active. MONAI Label is an open-source tool for labelling medical images. Vigil marked the KEV entry routine, not unusual. Both items are on Vigil's vulnerabilities page ↗ and in COP.

The sources it was checked against. Three public records, read again on 24 Nov 2026:

  • CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog, version 2026.11.24: added 23 Nov 2026 as a path traversal flaw that lets an unauthenticated remote attacker write files outside the intended directory, "potentially resulting in remote code execution". Federal agencies must act by 26 Nov 2026. Known ransomware campaign use: "Unknown".
  • The CVE record, published 20 Nov 2026: versions before 0.8.6 are affected, and its description says the flaw was exploited in the wild from September 2026. Its CVSS 3.1 base score is 9.8 (critical).
  • CISA's Vulnrichment assessment (SSVC), dated 20:42 UTC on 23 Nov 2026: exploitation active, automatable yes, technical impact total.

The exploit score, day by day

EPSS, from FIRST, is a model's probability that the flaw is exploited in the next 30 days, published daily. Vigil's item carries the score from the day CISA added the flaw; the next day's score was much higher.

DayEPSSPercentileNote
21 Nov0.03188thCVE published the day before
22 Nov0.03890th

24 Nov0.49899th

What this establishes

  • CISA lists the flaw as exploited in the wild and, separately, assesses exploitation as active. Vigil repeats CISA's statements without change. Both come from one publisher, so they are not two independent confirmations.
  • Why Vigil kept it routine. Vigil's EPSS alert needs known ransomware use and a current score of at least 0.5. CISA gives ransomware use as unknown, and the score Vigil held was 0.037. Even the 24 Nov score, 0.498, would not have met the rule.
  • How fast it moved: the CVE was published on 20 Nov and added to KEV three days later.

What it does not establish

  • Who exploited the flaw, against whom, or how widely. The KEV entry names no attacker and no victim.
  • That it has not been used for ransomware. "Unknown" means CISA does not know, not that it has not happened.
  • Why the EPSS score jumped on 24 Nov. FIRST publishes the scores, not the reason a score changed, and the score is a model's estimate, not a count of attacks.
  • How many MONAI Label servers can be reached from the internet. Vigil does not measure exposure.
  • That version 0.8.6 fixes the flaw. That comes from the CVE record; Vigil did not test it.

Check it yourself

  • KEV catalog: CISA's JSON feed ↗ (US public domain); search for CVE-2026-133968.
  • CVE record and CISA's assessment: Vulnrichment file ↗ (CC0); the SSVC options are under the "CISA ADP Vulnrichment" container.
  • EPSS: FIRST's API, with recent days ↗ (free to use with attribution).
  • Vigil's alert rule: the EPSS_ALERT rule in Vigil's feed code ↗.

Cordon: four governments' travel advice for Egypt, November 2026

The published finding. Cordon carries each government's advice as its own item and does not merge them. For Egypt on 25 Nov 2026 it listed four, each with the date that government last changed it:

  • cordon:advisory:EG, United States: "Level 2, Exercise Increased Caution" (24 Nov 2026).
  • cordon:fcdo:egypt, United Kingdom: "advises against all travel to parts and all but essential travel to parts" (27 Oct 2026).
  • cordon:canada:EG, Canada: "Exercise a high degree of caution (with regional advisories)" (12 Nov 2026).
  • cordon:safetravel:EG, New Zealand: "Exercise increased caution (other levels apply in some areas)" (31 Jul 2026).

The sources it was checked against. Each government's own published advice, read again on 24 Nov 2026: the US State Department's travel advisory data, updated 00:00 UTC on 24 Nov 2026; the FCDO's page through GOV.UK's content API, updated 16:03 UTC on 27 Oct 2026 ("Updated information about Regional tensions"); Global Affairs Canada's open data file for Egypt; and New Zealand's SafeTravel page, "Page updated 31 July 2026". Every date Cordon shows matches its source.

Where the advice agrees and where it differs

The overall levels look alike, but the regional advice under them does not. Wording is shortened from each source. New Zealand's regional detail was not checked for this case, so it is left out of the table.

What this establishes

  • What each government says and when it last changed it. Cordon reports the advice; it does not grade it.
  • All three governments in the table tell their citizens to stay out of North Sinai entirely.
  • Farther west they differ. On the Libyan border the band is 20 km for the UK and 50 km for Canada. For the Hala’ib Triangle the US says do not travel and the UK says against all but essential travel.

What it does not establish

  • That any area is more or less dangerous than another. Each government advises its own citizens, and that advice also reflects how much help its embassy can give there, so a stricter level is not proof of greater danger.
  • That the levels are on one scale. The US uses four numbered levels, and the UK states which areas it advises against. Canada and New Zealand each use their own wording. "Increased caution" from the US and "a high degree of caution" from Canada are not the same step.
  • That the advice is current everywhere. New Zealand's page was last changed in July, and the UK's in October. An older date does not mean the advice is wrong, only that it has not been revised since.
  • Why the governments differ. None of them publishes its reasoning beyond the advice itself.

Undertow: a YouTube takedown Google links to Russia, August 2026

The published finding. Undertow published UT-GOOGLE-0ff0742451: "Google: 32 YouTube channels terminated, linked to Russia (2026-08)". Undertow rates its confidence low. It names Armenia, Ukraine and the United States as countries in Google's description, but does not mark them as targets. The item is on Undertow ↗ and in COP.

The source it was checked against. Google's Influence Operations Bulletin for Q3 2026 ↗, published 30 Oct 2026 and read again on 24 Nov 2026. Under August it lists one entry with 32 YouTube channels terminated in an investigation Google links to Russia. That entry gives the languages (Armenian, Polish, Romanian, Russian and Ukrainian) and says which countries the content criticised: Armenia, Ukraine and the United States. Google allows reuse of these facts and counts but not of the bulletin's text, so this case does not quote it.

How Undertow reads the entry

What this establishes

  • Google says it terminated 32 YouTube channels in August 2026 as part of an investigation it links to Russia. Undertow's count, month and wording match the bulletin.
  • Why Undertow's confidence is low. The rubric it publishes caps confidence when there is only one line of evidence, and here it is Google's word alone. The evidence is easy to fake, and the method has no scored track record yet.

What it does not establish

  • Who directed the campaign. "Linked to Russia" is where Google places the operators, not a finding that the Russian state ran it.
  • That Armenia, Ukraine or the United States were targets. The bulletin says what the content criticised, not who it was aimed at, so Undertow does not put them on the map as targets.
  • How many people saw the content. Google gives no views or subscribers for this entry.
  • That it is part of any named operation. Google gives it no name, and no other publisher Undertow reads has reported it.
  • Anything about the channels themselves. They have been removed, so neither Undertow nor a reader can check them.

Check it yourself

  • The confidence rubric (tic-confidence-1.0): in the TIC doctrine kit ↗.
  • The item in COP's feed: today.geojson ↗; search for UT-GOOGLE-0ff0742451.

COP: an internet outage in Cameroon, read across three other programs, November 2026

The published finding. Blackout published blackout:radar-anomaly:3123fd7f-5639-46ed-9a0a-c37e71706282: a traffic anomaly on one Cameroonian network, AS15964 (CAMNET-AS), from 11:45 UTC to 13:30 UTC on 22 Nov 2026, which Cloudflare Radar flagged and marked verified. COP's outage triage then asked three other programs what they showed for Cameroon around that hour, and read the outage open. Elements had no significant hazard that began in the 72 hours before. Cordon had no travel or embassy notice issued or raised in the 48 hours before. Watchfloor's press attention was not raised. The reading is on COP ↗, under Outage triage.

The sources it was checked against. COP's own daily archive shows when the item arrived. It is absent from the snapshots of 23 Nov at 00:20, 06:21 and 12:20 UTC and present from the 18:20 UTC snapshot, about 29 hours after the anomaly ended, because Radar's verification came after the event. As a second instrument, IODA (Georgia Tech's Internet Outage Detection and Analysis) was asked for alerts on AS15964 and on Cameroon between 06:00 and 20:00 UTC on 22 Nov, on 25 Nov 2026. It returned none. The same query does return IODA's alerts for an outage that Blackout took from IODA (Tuvalu, 24 Nov), so the empty answer is IODA's, not a failed request.

What this establishes

  • Cloudflare saw an unusual drop in its own traffic from one Cameroonian network on 22 Nov 2026 and marked it verified. Blackout's item and COP's archive carry the same network, times and wording.
  • None of the context triage looks for was there: no new hazard, no fresh notice, no unusual press attention in Cameroon around that hour, with each layer reporting current data.
  • What "open" is worth. In Cameroon, almost every day reads open, and so did every documented Cameroonian shutdown of 2025. Here the reading adds little either way, and COP prints the base rate and the replay beside it to say so.

What it does not establish

  • The cause. Radar gives none, and triage never names one: "open" means nothing else in COP explains the drop, not that someone ordered it.
  • That it was a shutdown. A fault, maintenance or a power cut on one network looks the same in traffic.
  • That the country was affected. Cloudflare saw one network; the other layers are read for the whole country, and IODA showed no alert for the network or the country.
  • That nothing happened, from the empty layers. "Nothing found" covers only what those three programs read.
  • How it ends. The drafted question beside it (whether OONI measures blocking rising in Cameroon in the 72 hours after) is a draft, not a registered forecast, and has no probability.
